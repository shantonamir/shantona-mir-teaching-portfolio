BYU English Language Admissions Test L&S — WIP Research Overview

Full study background, findings, and test-design implications for the listening and speaking strand.

Shantona Mir · Brigham Young University · LTRC 2026

This page accompanies the WIP roundtable handout. It provides the full study background, findings, and test design implications for participants who want to dig deeper.

Why a BYU-Specific Test?

Standardized proficiency tests (TOEFL, IELTS, Duolingo) are designed for a global audience. They cannot capture the specific linguistic demands of a single institution — especially one with a distinctive religious and academic culture.

BYU's unique context:

  • ~500–800 international applicants per year
  • Lectures delivered in mainstream American English — lingua franca variation is less relevant
  • Religious course content (REL courses) uses vocabulary and registers absent from standard EAP corpora
  • Identity verification through church networks may offer more accessible pathways for some applicants
"Tests will never be as good as those tailored for a local context." — Jenkins & Leung, 2019

The cost problem: A single TOEFL/IELTS attempt costs $215–310 in the U.S. — high cost with low local validity.

BYU Strategic Plan alignment: Goal 1.E calls for admitting students committed to "the type of education described in the BYU mission and aims" and specifically calls for assessing the holistic admissions process to align more fully with that mission (Action 1.E.1).

Study Design — Present Situation Analysis (Qualitative Strand)

The broader project uses a mixed-methods needs analysis with two parallel tracks. This WIP reports the qualitative Present Situation Analysis (PSA) — what participants perceive about listening and speaking demands at BYU.

Data sources (14 total):

  • 11 faculty/TA sessions across 6 disciplines (REL, STAT, PSYCH, BIO, CS, ELING)
  • 3 focus group sessions with currently enrolled NNES students
  • Purposive sampling by NNES enrollment concentration

Coding protocol:

  • Inductive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006)
  • Frequency coding: theme present/absent per source (n=14)
  • Two-pass systematic transcript review per coder
  • Source-level salience coding — not raw mention count
  • Theme coded present only when meaningfully discussed

Findings — Listening and Speaking Themes

Three themes from the PSA are directly relevant to listening and speaking. Two are Tier I (universal demands) and one is Tier II (specific demand).

T2 — Spoken Academic Interaction & Participation (14/14 sources)

What courses demand:

  • Small-group discussions (2–3x per lecture in some courses)
  • Pair-and-share activities — explain concepts to a partner
  • Class-wide Q&A — spontaneous oral participation
  • Oral pass-offs to TAs — walk through a programming project (CS 111)
  • Weekly rotating group oral presentations (RelC 200)
  • Opening prayers in Religion courses — high-stakes, in front of class

What students experience:

  • Silent in whole-class settings, especially large lectures
  • Able to participate well in one-on-one or small groups
  • Confidence (not proficiency) cited as primary barrier
"Non-native English speakers are quiet during class. Everybody else is a riot." — CS 110 professor
"I feel like confidence is the thing they struggle with the most. Just having the confidence to raise their hand and speak in class." — RelC 200 professor

Asymmetric visibility: Faculty see silence as disengagement. Students experience it as cognitive overload + anxiety. This gap matters for test design.

T3 — Academic Listening & Lecture Comprehension (14/14 sources)

What courses demand:

  • Attending lectures — no phones/laptops in some courses; handwritten notes required
  • Processing fast-paced native-speed speech with topic shifts
  • Understanding peer explanations in group work (not just the instructor)
  • Following lab instructions and recitation discussions
  • Watching instructional videos (subtitles help but not always available)
  • Comprehending scripture readings aloud in Religion courses

BYU-specific demands:

  • Lectures in mainstream American English — not simplified or lingua franca-adapted
  • Gospel/religious register in REL courses (covenants, atonement, etc.)
  • Cultural references (80s/90s pop culture) embedded in lecture speech
"He's talking super fast. So sometimes I really have to concentrate a lot to catch up." — Focus Group 2 student
"More important is probably a comprehension of speech... spoken language comprehension is really, really important in my class." — BIO 100 professor
"My teacher bases everything on pop culture in the 80s and 90s. I can't understand what he's saying even though I'm catching every word." — Focus Group 2 student

Not on TOEFL / IELTS: Pragmatic/cultural comprehension and peer-speech listening are systematically excluded from standardized tests but emerged consistently across all 14 sources.

T10 — Speaking Anxiety & Participation Difficulties (10/14 sources)

Key patterns:

  • Nervousness intensifies with audience size — 100–200-person lectures
  • Translating before speaking causes partial expression (~60–70% of intended meaning)
  • Speaking exhaustion — sustained L2 processing is cognitively draining
  • Reluctance to speak spontaneously; prefer written or prepared forms
  • High-stakes speaking: opening class prayer, timed oral pass-offs
"I have something I want to talk, but because I have to change into the other language — English — I just can only express like 60 to 70%." — Focus Group 2 student
"Some days I'm feeling so exhausted — speaking in this vocabulary that requires more energy to understand makes me feel overwhelmed." — Focus Group 3 student

Asymmetric Visibility — Why Both Lenses Are Needed

Visible to faculty (graders/instructors):

  • Oral pass-offs and CS presentations — high-stakes interactive speaking
  • Silence during Q&A — read as disengagement, not anxiety
  • Argument coherence — whether spoken explanation is logical
  • Application transfer — the discriminator between strong and weak students

Visible to students (performers/learners):

  • Cognitive load of translating before speaking
  • Listening exhaustion from sustained L2 lecture processing
  • Pragmatic/cultural listening gaps — idioms, 80s pop culture, humor
  • Praying and reading scripture aloud — high-stakes, public, anxiety-inducing

Neither lens alone produces the full construct. Triangulation is not a methodological nicety — it is what makes the construct visible.

Implications for Test Design

Listening

What the test should include:

  • Lecture-paced, native-speed audio — not slowed or simplified
  • Topic shifts mid-lecture to simulate real classroom demands
  • Peer-explanation tasks: understanding a classmate, not just the instructor
  • BYU-specific registers: gospel terminology, academic discipline language
  • Cultural/pragmatic comprehension items (idiomatic, contextual meaning)

What the test should NOT overweight:

  • Simplified academic English (not representative of actual lectures)
  • Isolated word/phrase recognition without discourse context
  • Scripted, studio-recorded speech that lacks natural prosody

Speaking

What the test should include:

  • Interactive small-group or dyadic task — not just monologue
  • At least one prepared monologue to capture different register demands
  • Opinion/reflection prompt — culturally relevant topics
  • Spontaneous response task (simulate Q&A pressure)
  • Assessment of intelligibility + communicative effectiveness over accent

Construct considerations:

  • Anxiety is a construct concern, not just a test-taking condition — consider warmup tasks
  • Translation/processing time is real — design timing to allow natural production
  • Interactional competence (not fluency alone) is the target skill

Roundtable Discussion Questions

  1. How do we adapt a global test framework to a local context — which validity evidence is portable and which must be regenerated from scratch?
  1. The data show students can express only 60–70% of intended meaning due to L2 processing load. Is this a proficiency problem, a task design problem, or both?
  1. Speaking anxiety emerged strongly (10/14 sources). Should anxiety be part of the construct, or is it test-irrelevant variance to minimize?
  1. Peer-speech listening and cultural/pragmatic listening appeared across all 14 sources but are absent from TOEFL/IELTS. What would a valid BYU-specific listening task look like?
  1. What validation evidence would make the most compelling case for adopting a locally-developed test over TOEFL for institutional admissions?

Next Steps

Phase 1 completion:

  • Complete Target Situation Analysis: map listening/speaking tasks from 20 course syllabi to ACTFL proficiency levels
  • Triangulate PSA findings with TSA document data
  • Derive preliminary test specifications for Listening and Speaking sections

Phase 2 — Validation:

  • Build listening tasks from authentic BYU lecture samples (with permissions)
  • Design interactive speaking task + rater training protocol
  • Pilot with current ELC students; establish AI + human hybrid rating

Bigger questions:

  • Can a locally-developed test demonstrate equivalent predictive validity to standardized tests for first-year CGPA outcomes?
  • What is the right balance of BYU-specific content vs. generalizable academic language?
  • How do we design a speaking task that captures interactive competence — not just monologue fluency — within a standardized, ratable format?
